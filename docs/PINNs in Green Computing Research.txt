Critical Feasibility Assessment and Research Roadmap for Physics-Informed Neural Networks in Sustainable Computing
Executive Evaluation of the Proposed Shape Optimization Program
The proposed research program—leveraging Physics-Informed Neural Networks (PINNs) to automate hardware shape optimization for operating-energy reduction across data center heat sinks, electric vehicle (EV) battery cold plates, and industrial electric motors—represents a well-structured, intellectually honest, and methodologically sound blueprint for a twelve-week green computing course project1. By grounding the motivation in verifiable macro-level energy realities—specifically that global data centers consumed approximately 415 TWh in 2024 (roughly 1.5% of global electricity, projected to double by 2030 due to artificial intelligence workloads) with 30% to 40% dedicated to cooling at an average Power Usage Effectiveness (PUE) of 1.54, and that electric motor systems account for 53% of global electricity consumption (approximately 13,500 TWh)—the proposal establishes a clear bridge between scientific machine learning (SciML) and physical sustainability1.
The primary strength of the proposal lies in its scientific candor and defensive scoping1. In the broader computational physics literature, uncritical applications of PINNs frequently fail peer review by claiming superiority over mature numerical techniques without adequate baselines3. By proactively citing the benchmark findings of Grossmann et al. (2024)—which establish that the classical Finite Element Method (FEM) outperforms standard PINNs by one to three orders of magnitude in computation time and accuracy for low-dimensional forward partial differential equations (PDEs)—the project immunizes itself against the most common critique raised by domain specialists1. Rather than claiming a forward-solve speed advantage, the proposal frames its contribution around three defensible pillars: formulating an operating-energy proxy objective rather than optimizing purely for peak temperature or torque, translating relative geometric improvements into transparent order-of-magnitude annual kilowatt-hour (kWh) savings estimates with explicit assumptions, and logging the computational carbon footprint of the optimization loop itself via CodeCarbon1.
Furthermore, structuring the twelve-week execution timeline around a strict validation ladder—verifying code against a closed-form one-dimensional analytical fin equation before benchmarking against a custom 50-line NumPy Finite Difference Method (FDM) grid solver, and only attempting shape optimization once the PINN matches the reference solver within a few percent—ensures that every potential technical failure mode degrades gracefully into a valid academic deliverable1. However, while the proposal is highly feasible as an advanced course deliverable, transitioning this work into a peer-reviewed publication requires addressing several mathematical and physical bottlenecks inherent to two-dimensional (2D) steady-state conduction models and low-dimensional parametric sweeps2.
Granular Feasibility Analysis of the Three Hardware Options
Executing a PINN-based shape optimization loop within a twelve-week academic trimester on single-GPU cloud infrastructure (such as Google Colab with approximately 4 GB of active video memory) requires a strict architectural separation between two distinct neural frameworks referenced in the project plan: Parametric PINNs and Coordinate-Projection PINNs1.
In the realistic baseline architecture, a Parametric PINN feeds spatial coordinates    alongside a low-dimensional vector of explicit scalar geometric design knobs    (such as fin height, fin thickness, and fin spacing) into a single physics network—typically a six-layer multilayer perceptron (MLP) with 50 hyperbolic tangent (  ) units per layer—to predict the scalar temperature field   2. Once this network is trained across random collocation points sampled over both the spatial domain and the parameter box, automatic differentiation (  ) yields direct sensitivity gradients to drive gradient-based design updates over the shape knobs1. Conversely, the stretch architecture—modeled after the coordinate-projection framework of Zhang, Lin, and Wang (2024)—couples two separate neural networks in series: a shape neural network    that projects a fixed reference point cloud    (such as an initialized elliptical reference domain with a major axis of 1.5, minor axis of 0.7, and    inclination) into deformed physical coordinates    representing complex contours, and a physics field neural network    that solves the governing PDE over the evolving geometry simultaneously during training without external solver ground truth2.
While Zhang et al. (2024) demonstrated that coordinate projection can converge in roughly one hour on a single GPU using DeepXDE and PyTorch1, implementing simultaneous shape deformation and physics solving from scratch introduces severe topological stability challenges5. Specifically, the shape network must satisfy a strict positive Jacobian determinant constraint (  ) across the entire projected point cloud to preserve domain topology and prevent unphysical self-intersecting boundary deformations5. Consequently, prioritizing the single-network Parametric PINN for Weeks 5 through 9 and reserving joint coordinate projection as a stretch milestone is essential for guaranteeing on-time project completion1.

Option A represents the most secure path to a completed twelve-week deliverable1. The governing physics—steady-state 2D linear heat conduction (  ) within a homogeneous metallic domain subject to fixed temperature at the processor die interface, adiabatic insulation along symmetry boundaries, and convective heat loss (  ) along fin surfaces—is mathematically smooth and elliptic2. While CFD-driven cold plate optimization (Li et al., 2024; Guo et al., 2022) and forward PINN battery thermal simulations exist independently, unifying PINNs with cold plate geometric optimization under a dual objective—simultaneously minimizing peak cell temperature (  ) and cell-to-cell temperature spread (  ) under a pumping-power constraint—addresses both EV driving range and battery pack embodied carbon (via thermal-aging mitigation) while remaining directly applicable to liquid-cooled AI server racks1.

Worst-Case Pitfalls, Mathematical Bottlenecks, and Failure Modes
To prevent overclaiming and ensure rigorous experimental design, four critical failure modes must be anticipated and mitigated before implementation begins1.
1. The Low-Dimensional Amortization Trap and Classical Solver Dominance
The primary computational argument for training a neural surrogate in an engineering design loop is amortization: paying an upfront offline training cost to enable near-instantaneous online function evaluations and gradient queries across thousands of candidate geometries1. However, systematic benchmarks by Grossmann et al. (2024) demonstrate that for solving a 2D Poisson equation once, classical FEM is one to three orders of magnitude faster and significantly more accurate than a PINN; for nonlinear phase-field equations like Allen-Cahn, FEM is five to six orders of magnitude faster3. PINNs only achieve a computational advantage in online evaluation speed (two to three orders of magnitude faster than mesh interpolation in 3D) or when dimensionality scales to regimes where finite element meshes grow exponentially3.
In the proposed course project, optimizing    or    shape parameters (such as fin height and thickness) creates a severe amortization trap1. A 50-line vectorized NumPy FDM solver on a    grid solves the resulting sparse linear system (  ) in roughly 5 to 10 milliseconds on a standard laptop CPU2. Consequently, a brute-force grid sweep of    candidate geometries completes in under 30 seconds, and a three-parameter sweep of    geometries completes in under five minutes—whereas training the PINN requires roughly 60 minutes on a GPU1. Thus, at the 2-to-3 knob scale, the brute-force classical sweep will almost certainly beat the PINN in both wall-clock time and energy consumption (kWh)1. The project plan rightly anticipates this outcome and frames the head-to-head measurement as the core research comparison1. To demonstrate where the PINN actually adds value, the final report should explicitly calculate the amortization breakeven threshold (  ) and show how the curse of dimensionality causes grid sweeps to fail once parameter counts reach    or when continuous gradient-based sensitivity analysis is required2.

State of the Research: PINNs in Green and Sustainable Computing
To contextualize the proposed project and identify high-value avenues for thesis publication, it is necessary to examine how the scientific community currently deploys physics-informed machine learning across sustainable computing and energy systems. Because forward PDE solving in simple geometries is dominated by classical FEM3, recent high-impact publications concentrate on four domains where PINNs provide capabilities that mesh-based solvers cannot easily replicate: real-time facility control, electrochemical battery prognosis, inverse thermal tomography, and power grid optimization3.
1. Data Center Thermal Modeling and Safety-Constrained Cooling Control
In green cloud computing venues such as ACM e-Energy, IEEE Transactions on Sustainable Computing, and the IEEE International Green and Sustainable Computing Conference (IGSC), physics-informed networks are primarily deployed as fast, differentiable dynamics models inside closed-loop cooling and workload controllers15. Purely black-box Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) controllers can reduce data center cooling energy, but they rely on unsafe trial-and-error exploration and fail to generalize when server rack workloads or ambient weather shift15.
A landmark framework in this space is Phyllis (Wang et al., ACM e-Energy 2023), which embeds continuous-time thermodynamic differential equations governing data-hall air recirculation (  ) and two-stage chilled-water heat transfer into a lifelong reinforcement learning loop15. By regularizing the neural network with conservation of energy across Computer Room Air Conditioning (CRAC) units, chillers, pumps, and cooling towers (  ), Phyllis achieves 5.7% to 13.8% facility energy savings over feedback control while adapting 8x to 10x faster to non-stationary server changes with a maximum thermal overshoot of   15.
Subsequent investigations have expanded this paradigm across multiple architectural layers of the data center14. In commercial server hall deployments, physics-informed offline reinforcement learning frameworks now manage 80-dimensional sensor states and 22-dimensional Air Conditioning Unit (ACU) control actions (adjusting fan speeds    and chilled-water valve openings   ) using physics-guided reward functions that explicitly penalize cubic fan power consumption (  ) alongside soft-plus barrier penalties for cold-aisle thermal safety thresholds14. At the holistic facility level, multiphysics-informed optimization frameworks (Multi-PI Opt) trained solely on operational telemetry jointly model air-water heat transfer, CRAH fans, chillers, and pumps, reducing thermal prediction error margins to under 5% compared to 7% to 9% for purely data-driven models and enabling quadratic-programming safety layers that prevent server overheating during policy exploration19. Complementing these control policies, spatiotemporal Graph PINNs and ANN-CNN hybrid surrogates reconstruct 3D server room temperature fields up to 300,000 times faster than CFD simulations21, while dynamic thermal-aware job scheduling using physics-informed Proper Orthogonal Decomposition (POD)-Galerkin models (IEEE IGSC, 2024) jointly optimizes compute workload placement and cooling dissipation20.
2. Sustainable Energy Storage, Battery Degradation, and E-Waste Mitigation
Manufacturing lithium-ion battery packs for electric vehicles and cloud data center Uninterruptible Power Supplies (UPS) carries an immense embodied carbon and mineral extraction footprint2. Extending battery Remaining Useful Life (RUL), preventing premature disposal (e-waste), and certifying retired EV batteries for second-life stationary grid storage depend on accurate State of Health (SOH) estimation17. Purely data-driven regression models frequently produce physically impossible predictions—such as spontaneous capacity regeneration under noisy or random-walk load profiles—when evaluated outside their training distribution24.
Consequently, battery degradation modeling has become one of the most prolific and high-impact publishing arenas for PINNs17. Wang et al. (Nature Communications, 2024) developed PINN4SOH (with open-source code available on GitHub) to enforce stable degradation dynamics across varying aging regimes17, while Deng et al. (Applied Energy, 2025) introduced a generic physics-informed framework combining Solid Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) layer growth equations with dilated convolutional networks (SEI-DCN), achieving state-of-the-art RUL prediction accuracy using only four early-stage lifecycle data points17. Addressing late-life nonlinear failure, a 2025 Journal of Power Sources study introduced a transferable Deep Hidden Physics Model (DeepHPM) paired with 2D histogram feature engineering to model nonlinear degradation PDEs (  ), accurately detecting capacity "knee-onset" and predicting end-of-life knee points online across shifting usage protocols where lithium plating, Loss of Active Material (LAM), and resistance growth accelerate capacity fade28. In active battery control, Padisala et al. (2025) coupled a physics-informed RL agent with the open-source PyBaMM electrochemical simulator to estimate LAM in real time and dynamically adapt Constant-Current Constant-Voltage (CCCV) charging magnitudes to extend pack lifespan29. Similarly, recent work in Quality and Reliability Engineering International (2026) enforces first-order monotonic capacity decay laws via residual physics losses to stabilize SOH predictions in grid Battery Energy Storage Systems (BESS) operating under highly variable smart-grid control adjustments24.
3. Inverse Heat Transfer and Non-Invasive Diagnostics
While Grossmann et al. (2024) showed that PINNs struggle to beat FEM in forward problems, they explicitly noted that PINNs possess a structural advantage in inverse problems—where partial, noisy sensor observations are fused with governing PDEs to infer unknown physical properties or boundary conditions3. In classical FEM, solving an inverse problem requires deriving and iterating complex discrete adjoint equations or running thousands of forward Monte Carlo sweeps7. In a PINN, unknown parameters (such as thermal conductivity   , convective heat transfer coefficient   , or internal chip power dissipation   ) are simply treated as trainable tensors optimized alongside network weights in a single pass6.
Recent empirical studies confirm the precision of inverse PINNs under realistic measurement noise6. For boundary convective heat transfer coefficient (CHTC) reconstruction, a 2025 study demonstrated that an inverse PINN can infer both constant and spatially varying coefficients    from sparse temperature sensors under additive Gaussian noise up to 30%; for noise levels up to 10% and sampling rates of    or higher, the PINN matched conjugate heat transfer benchmarks with relative errors below 7.6%6. 4. Smart Grid AC Optimal Power Flow (AC-OPF)
In renewable energy integration and carbon-aware cloud computing, grid operators must solve the nonlinear, non-convex Alternating Current Optimal Power Flow (AC-OPF) equations every few minutes to dispatch fluctuating wind and solar generation without violating transmission line thermal limits16. Pioneering work by Nellikkath and Chatzivasileiadis (Electric Power Systems Research / IEEE Transactions on Power Systems, 2021–2023) and subsequent Physics-Informed Graph Neural Networks with hard constraints (PINCO, 2023–2024) embed power flow balance equations directly into the training loss or projection layers16. These models achieve millisecond-scale AC-OPF warm-starts and solutions, enabling high-frequency carbon intensity forecasting and real-time geographical load balancing across cloud data centers33.

Track 2: Physics-Informed Battery Degradation, SOH, and Capacity "Knee-Point" Prognosis for Cloud BESS and EVs
This track addresses the e-waste and embodied-carbon pillar of green computing by developing a PINN for predicting lithium-ion battery State of Health (SOH), Remaining Useful Life (RUL), and capacity knee-onset under realistic, variable load profiles such as data center UPS peak-shaving or EV driving cycles17. The neural network ingests partial charging voltage and current curves to predict capacity fade   , while the physics loss enforces a coupled first-order Solid Electrolyte Interphase (SEI) film growth differential equation and a strict monotonicity constraint (  ) so the network cannot hallucinate unphysical capacity recovery17.
Battery health prognosis represents one of the highest-impact publication arenas in sustainable energy (Nature Communications, Applied Energy, Journal of Power Sources)17. A focused study benchmarking how simplified SEI and Loss of Active Material (LAM) physics constraints enable accurate RUL and knee-point prediction using only four to ten early-life cycles—or under random-walk BESS operations—directly resolves critical challenges in data scarcity and predictive reliability17. Feasibility within a single trimester is exceptionally high: because degradation evolves over cycle time (  ) and 1D electrode depth (  ) rather than complex 2D or 3D spatial meshes, training completes in minutes on a free Colab GPU, and open-source starter repositories (PINN4SOH on GitHub27 and PyBaMM29) alongside public aging datasets (such as the 45-cell CLO validation benchmark23) eliminate the need to write reference PDE solvers from scratch.

	Strategic Recommendations and Execution Roadmap
To maximize both immediate academic grading success in the green computing course and long-term thesis publication impact, a dual-track execution strategy is recommended1. Between the three original proposals, Option B (EV and Data Center Liquid Cold Plate) offers the strongest combination of low-risk 2D Poisson physics and high application novelty, as it simultaneously serves two major sustainability narratives: EV battery lifetime extension and liquid-cooled AI data centers1. If maximum schedule safety is preferred, the team should initiate development with Option A (Data Center Heat Sink) during Weeks 1 to 6 to verify the 1D analytical fin formula and 2D FDM grid solver, and subsequently transition the validated codebase to Option B in Week 7 by reusing approximately 70% of the pipeline1. To elevate this course deliverable into a publishable manuscript, Track 1 (Inverse Sensing) should be integrated prior to running shape optimization: demonstrating that the PINN can reconstruct an unknown spatially varying convective cooling coefficient    from six noisy virtual temperature sensors establishes a concrete regime where the PINN decisively outperforms the classical grid solver3.
On the mathematical modeling front, the team must eliminate the "convection disconnect" and preempt gradient stiffness from the outset2. Rather than leaving the convective heat transfer coefficient    independent of fin or channel spacing    during optimization, embedding the analytical laminar channel resistance and Nusselt correlation into the Robin boundary loss ensures that narrowing the fluid channel automatically penalizes pressure drop (  ) and pumping or fan power (  )2. This refinement prevents the optimizer from collapsing into degenerate, infinitely thin fins and provides a physically rigorous kWh energy-saving calculation without solving Navier-Stokes equations2. Simultaneously, when constructing the PINN in Week 5, spatial coordinates and temperatures must be nondimensionalized to   , and the fixed chip or cell temperature boundary should be enforced via a hard output transformation rather than a soft penalty term, neutralizing the gradient pathologies documented by Wang et al. (2020) and Krishnapriyan et al. (2021)2.
Finally, if the overarching thesis aims for a top-tier Q1 journal or premier green computing conference, pairing the hardware shape-optimization study with Track 2 (Battery Degradation PINN) or Track 3 (Data Center Cooling Control) provides the strongest path to publication15. Utilizing open-source frameworks such as PINN4SOH and PyBaMM on empirical battery cycling datasets to curb e-waste27, or training a physics-informed rack thermal surrogate for safety-constrained workload scheduling in IEEE IGSC or ACM e-Energy15, grounds the thesis in real-world operational telemetry and completely bypasses the physical limitations of 2D synthetic geometry models.
